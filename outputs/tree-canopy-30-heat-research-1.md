## Findings

### Q1 — Specific canopy-cover percentages reported as thresholds or optima

**The cleanest direct "optimum %" result is 20–30% tree coverage.** Ouyang et al. (Hong Kong, validated ENVI-met) found a logarithmic, saturating cooling curve and defined **optimal cooling efficiency at 20–30% tree coverage ratio**, robust across low/mid/high urban densities and time of day [1]. A complementary Nanjing study quantified **maximum *effective* canopy thresholds that shrink as buildings get taller: 45% (11 floors), 30% (18 floors), 25% (33 floors)** — beyond these, extra trees add little temperature or comfort benefit, and in high-rise quarters trees barely relieve thermal discomfort [2]. A Chinese-language global study (229 cities, piecewise regression) reports **climate-zone-specific fractional-tree-cover thresholds of 19% / 30% / 45% / 55% (and 23% humid night)**, arguing thresholds should be set by local background climate [3]. The only city-wide *vegetation* breakpoint study found (Douala, Cameroon) puts the tipping point at **NDVI ≈ 0.33** (95% CI 0.316–0.342), beyond which vegetation switches from weak local to systemic LST cooling [4]; a four-Chinese-city threshold study reports comparable **NDVI ToCabs 0.31–0.39** plus neighboring-greenspace thresholds of 8.5–23.5% [5]. Related breakpoint literatures add: tree-patch **size** thresholds of ~0.6 and ~0.9 ha in hot-humid Asia [6], nonlinear (threshold-like) cooling efficiency in African cities [7], and an artificial-surface "turning point" of ~60–70% at which built cover overpowers tree cooling [8].

**Counter-evidence: several high-quality studies find linear, threshold-free relationships.** Tacoma field data (46 loggers, sidewalks) show strictly linear cooling of ~0.01 °C per 1% canopy within 10 m and explicitly conclude there is *no* clear canopy threshold at local scale — every added unit of cover helps; the authors contrast their result with Ziter 2019's >40% nonlinear finding [9]. Satellite-based cooling-efficiency studies also model linear per-% slopes: **0.063 °C per 1% fractional tree cover globally (0.087 in summer, ~0.007 at night)** [11] and **0.168 °C per 1% across 118 US cities (range 0.04–0.57)** [12].

**Inference for the review:** the literature does *not* support a single universal canopy percentage. Reported optima/thresholds cluster in the **20–45% range** (with street-level thermal-comfort metrics tending to reward *more* cover, up to 75% in Changchun [10]), and the "40% canopy" figure circulating in policy discourse traces back to Ziter 2019's nonlinearity (already covered by the parent), not to any independent replication. The 30% figure in the 3–30–300 rule is a planning benchmark (see Q3), and several authors explicitly argue canopy *percentage* is an insufficient metric [36].

### Q2 — Street trees: shade benefits vs. evaporative cooling

Across microclimate field studies, **shade (radiation blocking) dominates pedestrian-level heat-stress reduction, while air-temperature/evaporative effects are small and context-dependent**:
- Melbourne canyons: street-tree shade dropped **UTCI a full stress class** (very strong >38 °C → strong >32 °C) mainly through mean radiant temperature, while air cooling averaged only 0.2–0.6 °C (max 1.5 °C); cooling grows as canyon shallows [13].
- Changchun: with tree cover rising 13→35→75%, the frequency of strong heat stress (PET >35 °C) fell **64% → 11% → 0%**, with PET reductions of ~5–14 °C — far exceeding air-temperature changes [10].
- Tempe: shade (tree or artificial) lowered thermal sensation ~1 scale point; PET neutral at 28.6 °C [16].
- Istanbul: trees cut PET 5–6 °C under El Niño heat, though marginal under the most extreme stress [19].
- Utrecht is the cleanest "mechanism split" study: **10% tree crown cover reduced Tmrt ~1 K with no significant air-temperature effect** — i.e., the benefit is radiative, not evaporative [17].
- Shade quality is driven by **canopy density / Plant Area Index**, not cover % alone [14][32]; and shade is fragile — Melbourne London Planes lost **30–50% of canopy during a heatwave**, eroding the cooling service exactly when needed [15].
- Perceived comfort responds strongly to street-tree shade (Montevideo: >2× the share of people comfortable under shade) [18].

**Evaporative caveats (important counterpoint):** in Cairo's low-density districts, modeled tree planting (30–50% + 70% grass) **raised air temperature by ~3 K via added humidity**, while cutting PET only 1–3 K — trees can thermally disappoint where evaporation dominates and shade is diffuse [20]. This mirrors Rahman et al.'s meta-analytic finding that transpirative cooling is ~10× weaker over paved tree pits than over grass [32]. Net implication: canopy targets aimed at *thermal comfort* should be shade- (SVF/LAI-) oriented, and canopy targets aimed at *air temperature* should not expect more than ~0.06–0.17 °C per 1% cover [11][12].

### Q3 — 2023–2026 studies on the 3–30–300 rule / 30% canopy

- **Konijnendijk 2023** is the origin of the 3–30–300 rule — explicitly framed as an *evidence-based planning guideline* for equitable nature access rather than a measured cooling optimum [26].
- **Browning et al. 2024** (STOTEN) is the methodological consensus on how to measure each component (high-res land cover for the "30"; street-view/window analyses for the "3") [27].
- **Croeser et al. 2024** (Nat. Commun.) applied the rule to **2.5 million buildings across 8 global cities** (NYC, Amsterdam, Buenos Aires, Denver, Sydney, Melbourne, Seattle, Singapore): only **Singapore (~75%) and Seattle (~45%)** clear the 30% neighborhood-canopy benchmark; most cities fail on canopy, not on seeing trees or park access [28]. (Press coverage of this study quotes the authors saying "~40% canopy cover is needed to substantially lower daytime air temperatures," citing the Ziter-type evidence — secondary claim [see ScienceDaily, https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/11/241119132943.htm].)
- **Owen et al. 2024** (Urban For. Urban Green.): none of Paris, Aarhus, or Velika Gorica meets all 3-30-300 components; reaching 30% neighborhood green would require converting 10–18.4% of urban footprint, with >100,000 buildings and >900,000 residents affected; flags methodological ambiguity, green-gentrification, and street-canyon pollution risks [29]. A Warsaw district-level implementation case study adds a European application [30].
- **Rahman et al. 2024** (Landsc. Urban Plan., perspective) directly critiques canopy-% targets — canopy quality, water-use strategy, and site climate matter as much as the number; contextualizes 3-30-300's 30% and the EU Nature Restoration Law's 10% minimum tree cover [36].

### Heat-related mortality / health (supplementary angle)

- **Kalkstein et al. 2022 (LA County):** aggressive tree + albedo interventions could save **~1 in 4 heat-wave deaths** (baseline urban tree cover 16.6%; modeled mortality cuts 18–29% per event) [21].
- **Sinha et al. 2022 (10 US cities):** **+10% tree cover** cut estimated heat mortality by up to **22% (Phoenix)**; NYC ~3,800 fewer deaths; drier/hotter cities benefit most per capita [22].
- **Chi et al. 2025 (6.2 M Swiss adults):** canopy cover (HR 0.979 per 12.4% IQR) is protective, but **configuration matters more**: aggregated/connected canopies HR 0.831, fragmented HR 1.073 — "more canopy, but arranged" [23].
- **Song et al. 2023 (Hong Kong):** at extreme heat, high **eye-level street greenery** erased the heat-mortality risk (RR 0.943 vs 1.103 in low-greenery areas); street-view greenery outperformed NDVI/greenspace-% as a modifier — supporting shade-at-eye-level over overhead canopy % [24].
- Sydney modeling study examines tree-cover mitigation of UHI-amplified heatwave mortality (title-level; details not read) [25].

### Cross-cutting inference
When the review team writes about canopy thresholds, the defensible synthesis is: (i) **no single % threshold** — physical optima cluster at 20–45% and depend on metric (LST/air temp vs PET/UTCI vs mortality), climate, and urban density [1][2][3][5][11]; (ii) **shade metrics (canopy density/LAI/SVF, eye-level greenery) outperform cover-% as predictors of human heat-stress outcomes** [14][17][24][31]; (iii) at the population-health scale, **+10% canopy** and canopy *configuration* are the quantifiable levers [22][23]; (iv) the 30% benchmark is a policy/equity target whose evidence base is best documented by Croeser 2024 and Owen 2024 [28][29].
